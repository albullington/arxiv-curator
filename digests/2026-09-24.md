## [Agent-Editing World Model: Rethinking World Modeling for LLM Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28416v1)
**Score:** 0.900  |  **arXiv:** 2609.28416v1

Rather than predicting environment observations, this paper introduces the Agent-Editing World Model (AEWM), a framework that prevents compounding errors by actively identifying and editing flawed reasoning and actions out of an agent’s interaction history. Implemented via an execution framework called EditAct, it directly revises the agent's underlying task state instead of merely providing passive critiques. Across search, terminal, and software engineering domains, this approach boosts performance by 3.2–6.7 points over strong baselines and produces higher-quality trajectories for offline fine-tuning.

## [Memory Attention](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28399v1)
**Score:** 0.894  |  **arXiv:** 2609.28399v1

The authors introduce Memory Attention (MA), an alternative attention mechanism that replaces standard value projections with a combination of token-indexed memory tables and contextual keys to capture reusable token-level information. This design enables CPU offloading with prefetching to reduce GPU memory footprint and simplifies inference-time value computation to table lookups and additions. Experiments demonstrate that MA achieves better language modeling and downstream task performance compared to standard attention models under matched training budgets.

## [When and Where to Trust the Teacher: Unifying On-Policy Distillation and GRPO through Entropy-Calibrated Credit Assignment](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28385v1)
**Score:** 0.867  |  **arXiv:** 2609.28385v1

This paper introduces **UECR-GRPO**, a framework designed to better combine sparse verifier rewards (RLVR) with dense teacher guidance (on-policy distillation) for mathematical reasoning. To fix credit assignment issues in existing hybrids, it integrates teacher signals with verifier rewards before group normalization at the response level and redistributes token-level credit calibrated by teacher entropy to downweight uncertain feedback. Evaluated across five math reasoning benchmarks on Qwen3-1.7B and 4B models, the approach consistently outperforms existing RLVR and distillation baselines.

## [Can LLMs Reason About Runtime Behavior? A Repository-Level Dynamic Benchmark](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28449v1)
**Score:** 0.864  |  **arXiv:** 2609.28449v1

This paper introduces **SWE-Flux**, a benchmark of 480 instances across 12 Python repositories that evaluates how well LLMs reason about dynamic, repository-level runtime behavior using ground truth gathered directly from instrumented test executions. Evaluated models struggled significantly—peaking at only 37% accuracy—failing primarily on inter-procedural execution, dataflow, and precise program state tracking despite managing simpler, localized control flow. Additionally, the authors demonstrate an automated input-perturbation pipeline that can generate fresh, even more challenging benchmark variants to mitigate contamination and continuously test model robustness.

## [Order-Invariant Answers, Order-Sensitive Representations in Mathematical Reasoning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28442v1)
**Score:** 0.857  |  **arXiv:** 2609.28442v1

This paper investigates whether language models that consistently solve mathematical problems regardless of input rule order also maintain order-invariant internal representations. Counterintuitively, tests across 16 models (1B–8B parameters) show that higher accuracy strongly correlates with *more distinct* internal representations for different rule orders, rather than invariant ones. These findings demonstrate that models can achieve order-invariant answers through order-sensitive internal states, challenging the assumption that robust reasoning requires representation invariance.

## [Learning Collective Dynamics with Differentiable Gaussian Representations](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28405v1)
**Score:** 0.856  |  **arXiv:** 2609.28405v1
**Length:** 20 pages

This paper introduces Differentiable Gaussian Dynamics (DGD), an end-to-end differentiable framework designed to model evolving collective behavior using only aggregate count data rather than individual-level tracking. The method couples a Gaussian mixture representation of individual differences with temporal feedback recurrence, allowing aggregate prediction errors to jointly optimize the latent population distribution and its response dynamics. Across real-world recommendation and retail benchmarks, DGD significantly outperforms adapted deep time-series baselines (like DeepAR), demonstrating clear utility for researchers modeling population dynamics under aggregation or privacy constraints.

## [StudentBench: AI and human tutoring yield equivalent GRE learning gains](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28470v1)
**Score:** 0.853  |  **arXiv:** 2609.28470v1
**Length:** 47 pages

The authors introduce StudentBench, an evaluation platform used to compare LLM tutoring against expert human tutoring across 2,383 GRE students. They found that AI tutoring produces learning gains statistically equivalent to human tutoring—even outperforming humans in five of seven tested domains—at over 900 times lower cost. The paper also benchmarks AI models across lesson planning, problem generation, and conversational dynamics, showing that faster AI response times directly correlate with higher engagement and improved learning gains.

## [Fine-Tuning LLMs for Translation: General Forgetting Mitigation Does Not Preserve MT-Specific Instruction Following](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28395v1)
**Score:** 0.838  |  **arXiv:** 2609.28395v1

While standard catastrophic forgetting mitigations (such as Elastic Weight Consolidation) effectively preserve an LLM's general capabilities during translation fine-tuning, they fail to retain machine-translation-specific instruction following, such as controlling formality, gender, or length. The authors show that only explicitly mixing control-task examples into the training data preserves these specific capabilities, though even this approach fails to generalize to unseen prompts for the same task.

## [Context-Continuous Preference Learning for Exoskeleton Personalization](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28427v1)
**Score:** 0.832  |  **arXiv:** 2609.28427v1

To reduce the time and user fatigue required to personalize exoskeletons across multiple tasks, the authors introduce Context-Continuous Preference Learning (CCPL), a Gaussian process model that shares feedback across similar operating conditions. In retrospective human evaluations on ankle and elbow exoskeletons, CCPL reduced the required feedback budget by 17% to 37% compared to learning conditions independently, provided user preferences changed smoothly. This paper is particularly relevant for researchers working on sample-efficient human-in-the-loop optimization, though real-time online validation in humans remains future work.
