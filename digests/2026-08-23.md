## [Which Eviction Policy Should an LLM Cache Use? A Systematic Study Across Workloads, Capacities, and Encoders](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20280v1)
**Score:** 0.894  |  **arXiv:** 2608.20280v1
**Length:** 11 pages

This systematic evaluation reveals that no semantic cache eviction policy meaningfully outperforms standard Least Frequently Used (LFU)—improving on it by at most 0.041 percentage points—making LFU the strongest simple default. More critically, the authors discover a severe validity issue: typical similarity thresholds yield answer-substitutable responses only 2.1–3.9% of the time, causing raw hit rates of 51–60% to plunge to quality-adjusted rates of 1.1–2.2%. Ultimately, the paper demonstrates that ensuring answer validity and tuning non-transferable encoder thresholds are far more vital for LLM caching than fine-tuning eviction policies.

## [Learning When to Think: Adaptive Reasoning for Test-Time Compute Allocation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20256v1)
**Score:** 0.883  |  **arXiv:** 2608.20256v1

This paper presents an end-to-end reinforcement learning approach that enables reasoning models to dynamically allocate test-time compute by selecting a reasoning budget—direct answer, short reasoning, or long reasoning—as their first generated token without needing a separate router. Evaluated on a 1.5B parameter model, this strategy effectively sorts problems by difficulty, preserving near-baseline accuracy on MATH500 while cutting average token generation by 41%. The learned allocation also transfers zero-shot to other benchmarks, yielding up to a 76% token reduction on easier tasks like GSM8K.

**Score:** 0.877  |  **arXiv:** 2608.20338v1
**Length:** 17 pages

The authors introduce **ConceptGuard**, a new benchmark designed to evaluate context-sensitive LLM unlearning for "dual-use" concepts—requiring models to eliminate harmful applications of an idea while preserving its benign, useful contexts. Unlike existing benchmarks that only test disjoint, fact-level forgetting, ConceptGuard evaluates whether methods can achieve true intent- and concept-level separation across complementary retain and forget sets. Experiments reveal that current unlearning techniques perform poorly under this setup, exposing weak contextual control and steep trade-offs between safety and utility.

## [Phantom Gains: Auditing Self-Improvement Against a Measured Null](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20290v1)
**Score:** 0.873  |  **arXiv:** 2608.20290v1

This paper demonstrates that standard methods for tracking problem-level gains and losses in LLM self-training are vulnerable to measurement artifacts (such as batching noise) that routinely manufacture false capability improvements. By introducing a false-discovery-rate–controlled exact test against a measured null baseline, the authors show that self-training fails to unlock rare capabilities—unlike external distillation—and frequently corrupts previously solved problems. Researchers should read this paper for a rigorous, cost-effective statistical framework to audit self-improvement and avoid reporting phantom gains.
